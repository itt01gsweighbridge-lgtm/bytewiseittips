Router marketing is full of big numbers and confusing names: AX5400, BE19000, tri-band, mesh. This guide explains what actually matters so you can pick a router that suits your home and devices. It compares technologies and published specifications rather than reviewing individual products.

## Step 1: Understand the Wi-Fi standards

| Name | Technical name | Bands | Key features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wi-Fi 5 | 802.11ac | 5 GHz (with 2.4 GHz via older standard) | Older but still common |
| **Wi-Fi 6** | 802.11ax | 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz | Better performance with many devices (OFDMA), target wake time for battery saving, WPA3 required for certification |
| **Wi-Fi 6E** | 802.11ax | Adds **6 GHz** | Extra, less-congested spectrum for compatible devices |
| **Wi-Fi 7** | 802.11be | 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz | Wider 320 MHz channels in 6 GHz, Multi-Link Operation (MLO) using several bands at once, higher peak speeds |

Routers are backwards compatible, so older devices still connect – they just use the older standard’s capabilities.

> **Your devices matter as much as the router.** A Wi-Fi 7 router won’t make a Wi-Fi 5 laptop faster than Wi-Fi 5 allows. Check what your phones, laptops and TVs support.

Availability of the 6 GHz band and allowed power levels vary by country, so check that 6E or 7 features are supported where you live.

## Step 2: Decode the speed numbers

A router labelled **AX5400** or **BE9300** is advertising the *combined theoretical maximum* of all its bands added together. No single device will ever reach that figure – a typical phone or laptop connects to one band with a limited number of antennas. Use these numbers to compare models within the same family, not as a promise of real-world speed.

What matters more in practice:

- whether it supports the bands your devices use;
- coverage in your particular home;
- the number of devices it can handle well;
- software quality, updates and security.

## Step 3: Single router or mesh?

- **Single router:** fine for flats and smaller homes where the router can be placed centrally.
- **Mesh system:** two or more units that work together as one network, ideal for larger homes, multiple floors, thick walls or long, narrow layouts.
- **Wired backhaul:** if you can connect mesh units to each other with Ethernet cables, performance improves significantly. Look for mesh units with Ethernet ports.
- **Tri-band mesh:** some mesh systems use an extra band dedicated to communication between units, which helps when there’s no wired backhaul.

If you’re fighting dead zones, see also our guide to [improving home Wi-Fi](/articles/improve-home-wifi-speed/).

## Step 4: Check ports and wired features

- **WAN port speed:** if your broadband plan is faster than 1 Gbps, you need a router with a multi-gig WAN port (2.5 Gbps or more).
- **LAN ports:** enough Ethernet ports for desktops, consoles, TVs and network storage – and multi-gig LAN ports if you need fast local transfers.
- **USB port:** some routers can share a printer or drive, though dedicated network storage is usually better.

## Step 5: Prioritise security and updates

A router is the front door of your network. Look for:

- **WPA3** support (with WPA2/WPA3 mixed mode for older devices);
- **automatic firmware updates**, and a manufacturer with a clear record of providing security updates for several years;
- the ability to change the **admin password** (and no fixed default passwords);
- a **guest network** to keep visitors and smart home devices separate from your main devices;
- the option to disable remote management and WPS if you don’t need them.

When a router stops receiving security updates, it’s time to replace it.

## Step 6: Consider useful extras

- **Parental controls** and scheduling.
- **Quality of Service (QoS)** to prioritise video calls or gaming.
- **An easy app** for setup and monitoring.
- **VPN server or client** features for advanced users.
- **Subscriptions:** some features (advanced security, parental controls) require a paid subscription – check what’s included without one.

## Quick recommendations by situation

- **Small flat, few devices:** a quality Wi-Fi 6 router is usually plenty.
- **Family home with many devices:** Wi-Fi 6 or 6E; consider mesh for multiple floors.
- **Large or multi-storey home:** a mesh system, ideally with wired backhaul.
- **Latest phones and laptops, very fast broadband:** Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7 with a multi-gig WAN port.
- **Small office:** look for business-oriented features such as VLANs, multiple SSIDs, reliable updates, and consider professional access points.

## Conclusion

Choose a router based on your home’s size, your devices and your broadband speed rather than the biggest number on the box. Wi-Fi 6 is a solid baseline, 6E and 7 add capacity for newer devices, and mesh solves coverage problems in larger homes. Above all, pick a manufacturer that provides regular security updates.
