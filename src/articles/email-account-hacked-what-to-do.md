Your friends are getting strange messages from your address, you've had a "new sign-in" alert you don't recognise, or your password suddenly stops working. These are classic signs that someone else has got into your email account.

It's worth acting quickly. Your email is usually the key to everything else: whoever controls it can reset the passwords for your shopping, social media and sometimes banking accounts. This guide walks you through the steps recommended by the UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), Google and Microsoft, in a sensible order, so you can take back control and make sure the intruder can't quietly get back in.

## Signs your email account has been hacked

According to the NCSC and Google, common warning signs include:

- You can't sign in, or your password has been changed.
- Security settings such as your recovery phone number or email address have changed.
- Contacts receive spam or scam messages from you, or you find sent emails you didn't write.
- You stop receiving email, or messages are missing and not in the deleted items or bin.
- Sign-in alerts from unfamiliar locations, devices or at unusual times.
- Purchases or money transfers you didn't make.

One sign on its own isn't proof – a friend may simply have received a spoofed email that only *looks* as though it came from you. But if you see several of these, assume the account is compromised and work through the steps below.

## Step 1: Check your device for malware first

Before you type a new password, make sure the computer you're using is clean. If a keylogger or other malware is installed, it could capture the new password straight away. Microsoft's recovery guidance puts this step first.

On Windows 11, run a full scan with Microsoft Defender:

1. Open **Windows Security** from the Start menu.
2. Select **Virus & threat protection**.
3. Select **Scan options**, choose **Full scan**, then **Scan now**.

A full scan can take a while. If you use other security software, make sure it is up to date and run its full scan instead. If you suspect the device itself is compromised, use a different device you trust for the next steps.

## Step 2: Sign in and change your password

If you can still sign in, change your password immediately. Use a long, unique password you have never used anywhere else – the NCSC suggests three random words as a good starting point. A [password manager](/articles/strong-passwords-password-manager/) makes this much easier.

If you can't sign in, go to your provider's official recovery page rather than following links in emails:

- **Gmail / Google:** the Google Account recovery page at accounts.google.com/signin/recovery. Answer the questions as accurately as you can.
- **Outlook.com / Microsoft account:** use the **Forgot password** link on the sign-in page, or Microsoft's account recovery form if that doesn't work.

> **Warning:** Never use a phone number or "support" link from a search advert, social media comment or unexpected message. Fake recovery services are a common scam. Type the provider's address yourself.

Next, change the password on any other account where you used the same or a similar password. Attackers routinely try stolen passwords on other sites.

## Step 3: Remove forwarding, filters and rules the attacker added

This is the step most people miss. The NCSC warns that criminals often set up forwarding so they receive a copy of every email you get – including password reset links. Your new password won't stop that.

**In Gmail**, open Settings and check:

- **Forwarding and POP/IMAP** – remove any forwarding address you don't recognise, and turn off IMAP or POP access if you don't use it.
- **Filters and Blocked Addresses** – delete filters that forward, delete or archive messages automatically.
- **Accounts** – check **Grant access to your account** (mail delegation) and the "send mail as" addresses.
- Your **vacation responder** (automatic reply), signature and any scheduled emails.

**In Outlook.com**, select **Settings** (the gear icon), then **Mail** > **Forwarding**. If **Enable forwarding** is ticked and you didn't set it up, clear it and select **Save**. Microsoft also advises checking your automatic replies and connected accounts, and you should review your mail rules for anything that forwards or deletes messages.

## Step 4: Sign out other devices and review recent activity

Signing out all other sessions forces anyone using your account to enter the new password.

- **Google:** go to myaccount.google.com and select **Security & sign-in**. Under **Recent security events**, choose **Review security events** and select **No, it wasn't me** for anything you don't recognise. Then, under **Your devices**, choose **Manage devices** and sign out any device that isn't yours.
- **Other services:** look in the app or website's **Settings**, **Privacy** or **Account** menu for an option to sign out of all devices, as the NCSC recommends.

While you're there, remove any third-party apps or services with access to your account that you don't recognise.

## Step 5: Check your recovery details

Attackers often add their own phone number or backup email so they can reset your password again later. Check that your **recovery phone number** and **recovery email address** are yours and up to date, and remove anything you don't recognise. Google's guidance specifically lists the recovery phone, recovery email and "apps with access to your account" as settings to review after suspicious activity; Microsoft account holders will find their security info in the Microsoft account's security settings.

## Step 6: Turn on two-step verification

Two-step verification (2SV, also called 2FA or MFA) means a stolen password alone is no longer enough to get in. The NCSC recommends turning it on for email first, then banking and other important accounts. An authenticator app, passkey or security key is stronger than SMS codes. Our [two-factor authentication guide](/articles/two-factor-authentication-guide/) explains each option and how to keep backup codes so you don't lock yourself out.

## Step 7: Warn your contacts and check your finances

- **Tell your contacts** that your account was hacked and ask them to treat recent messages from you with suspicion – especially any asking for money, gift cards or for them to open a link.
- **Check bank statements and online shopping accounts** for transactions you didn't make. Contact your bank using the number on your card or its official website.
- **Report it:** in the UK, if you've lost money, tell your bank straight away and report it to Report Fraud (Police Scotland if you're in Scotland). Elsewhere, use your national fraud reporting service.

## Step 8: Update your devices and stay alert

Install the latest updates for your operating system, browser and apps, and turn on automatic updates. Many hacks start with a convincing fake sign-in page, so learn to [recognise phishing emails](/articles/recognise-phishing-emails/) – and never enter your email password from a link in a message.

## If you can't recover the account

Sometimes recovery isn't possible – for example, if the attacker changed every recovery option. Keep trying the official recovery process from a device and network you've used before. If it still fails, the NCSC advises creating a new account, telling your contacts you no longer use the old one, and updating your bank, utility and shopping accounts with the new address.

## Conclusion

A hacked email account is stressful, but it is usually recoverable if you act methodically. Scan your device, change your password, then hunt down the things that let an attacker back in: forwarding rules, filters, recovery details and signed-in devices. Finish by turning on two-step verification and using unique passwords, and your account will be far harder to take over again.
